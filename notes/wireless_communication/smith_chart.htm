On the beauty of Smith Chart

Since the time I did my first recitation for the undergraduate students in the course of electromagnetics in the University of New Hampshire, I always wanted to make a piece to pay a tribute to this interesting and elegant effort of my peers for offering a computation framework which beautifully makes use of graphs and geometry with calculus to solve a typical problem in applied complex numbers. In this page I would take the liberty to describe when, how, and why we end up with the smith chart when working with radio frequency (RF) signals.

In practice, RF signals are used in a variety of different applications, from information to energy transfer systems. Regardless of the application, we need to bring the generated RF signals from one point to another like from transmitter amplifier to the radiating antenna, or from a satellite receiver dish to the tuner, and so on and so forth. Since the RF signals have alternating temporal behavior we would need to model the whole connection from the signal source to the end point in terms of impedances. However, since almost all the RF signals are modulated steady state sinusoid signals, there are standard elements to model such an end-to-end model. It is straighfoward to show that for a typical RF circuit with atransmission line (e.g., a PCB trace), there will be two components on the transmission line given by: \[ V(z,t) = V_+ e^{-\gamma z} + V_{-} e^{\gamma z} \] \[ I(z,t) = I_{+}e^{-\gamma z} - I_{-} e^{\gamma z} \] where the \(+\) and \(-\) sings distinguishes such forward and backward traveling components. I have created a dedicated page where I go over the mathematics and modeling of the tranmssion lines [here]. The above two equations are derived there and provide more explanations and details on how we end up with these two characterizing equations. Here, we can treat the backwarding signal as a reflected component and come up with a reflection parameter as \[ \Gamma = \frac{V_{-}}{V_{+}}e^{2\gamma z} \] Thus, if we define \(Z_0\) as the interinsic impedence of the transmission line where both forward and backward going components will experience, replacing the backward component using the reflection parameter, we can write: \[ \frac{V(z,t)}{I(z,t)} = \frac{V_{+}e^{-\gamma z} + \Gamma V_{+}e^{\gamma z}e^{-2\gamma z}}{V_{+}e^{-\gamma z} - \Gamma V_{+}e^{\gamma z}e^{-2\gamma z}}Z_0 \longrightarrow Z_L= \frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}Z_0 \longrightarrow \Gamma = \frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0} \]

Smith Chart

In practice, the transmission lines are very efficient that can be considered lossless which allows for a powerful RF framework for system design. For ease of use, an interesting graphic-based tool can be developed in this framework which is known as the Smith Chart! Particularly, due to the lossless assumption, \(\gamma = j\beta \), any traverse along the transmission line would result in phase change. This enables us to overcome several engineering challenges in the design of RF systems. For example, finding the input impedance for the combined load and transmission line or matching the load impedance like the antenna to the transmission line impedance to maximize the energy transfer to the antenna. But what is the smith chart? The Smith Chart is a series of polar plots that shows the values of reflection parameter for different values of the normalized load. Perhaps, it is more tractable to follow this mathematically. From the definition of the reflection parameter above, if we normalize the load impedance by the intrinsic impedance, we can come up with a series of polar plots as \[ z_l = \frac{Z_L}{Z_0} \longrightarrow x + jy = \frac{1 + \Gamma_r +j\Gamma_i}{1 - \Gamma_r +j\Gamma_i} \longrightarrow x = \frac{1 - \Gamma_r^2 -\Gamma_i^2}{\left(1-\Gamma_r\right)^2 + \Gamma_i^2} \quad y = \frac{2\Gamma_i}{\left(1-\Gamma_r\right)^2 + \Gamma_i^2} \] As can be seen, for any value of the normalized load, we get a reflection parameter where its real and imaginary parts both are coupled with the real and imaginary parts of the normalized load in a second order fashion. This implies that we can hove a second order system of equations to solve for the value of \(\Gamma_r\) and \(Gamma_i\) for any specific value of \(z_L\). Particularly, after some manipulations, the following system of equations can be obtained \begin{align} \left(\Gamma_r - \frac{x}{1+x}\right)^2 + \Gamma_i^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\Gamma_r + 1}\right)^2 \quad \quad \left(\Gamma_r - 1\right)^2 + \left(\Gamma_i - \frac{1}{y}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{y}\right)^2 \end{align} Therefore, if we create a coordinate with \(\Gamma_r\) as the vertical axis and the \(\Gamma_i\) as the horizontal, we can plot each equation for a collection of different arbitrary values of \(x\) and \(y\). If we keep adding these curves, we can populate the reflection coordinate space as illustrated in the figure below: Furthermore, as I said earlier, traversing the transmission line would result in phase shift of the voltage. This feature is also included in the chart on the circumference in a very interesting way! Particularly, using the definition of the reflection parameter above, we can characterize the angular rotation around the center of the reflection coordinate as: \[ \Psi = \tan^{-1}(\frac{\mathcal{Im}\{\Gamma e^{-j\beta z} \}}{\mathcal{Re}\{\Gamma e^{-2j\beta z}\}}) \] So, as we move from the load to the source which is called `wavelength towards generator`, we travel around the smith chart in the clock wise order since we are adding a negative phase of \(2\beta l\) depending on the distance \(l\). So, we complete one circumnavigation when the \(\beta l = \pi \). On the Smith Chart's circumference there are two phase scales which show increment of phase for `wtg` and `wtl` travel where the length of the travel is normalized by the wavelength.

Application examples

One of the most common application of the Smith Chart is impedance matching in RF circuits to maximize energy transfer from the radio source to the antenna. Suppose we have a transmission line with intrinsic impedance of \(Z_0= 50\Omega\) that is terminated with an antenna whose impedance is \(Z_L=35 + j40 \Omega \). Pinpointing the normalized load impedance on the Smith Chart, we can draw a line from the center of the chart to the point where the line length will be equal to \(|\Gamma|\). Next we draw a circle from the center and for a radius equal to \(|\Gamma|\). This circle will show our phase as we traverse along the transmission line. Here, to get a perfect match, we want to see at what location on the transmission line, we can have a normalized impedance with the real part equal to 1. At that point, would then add a canceling impedance to make the normalized load impedance equal to 1 which results in \(\Gamma = 0\) or a perfect matching.

There is also another case for using Smith Chart in impedance matching context. In this case, the matching occurs at the load such that we add a passive circuit to compensate for the unbalanced normalized load. Particularly, in this approach, we no longer traverse the transmission line, but we move directly from the \(z_L\) to the center of the chart by adding a passive \(\pi\) or L-section circuit before the load where the lump elements of the compensatory circuit is chosen to move the normalized load to the center.

It is worth mentioning that, there is a quantity called voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR) which essentially describes how well the transmission line is matched with the load impedance in that, it is basically shows how much power is dissipated due to backward going component. This quantity is defined as \[ \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|} \] Furthermore, there is another similar quantity called `returned loss` which characterize the wasted power in reflection: \[ \text{RL} = -20 \log_{10}\left(|\Gamma|\right) \ \ \text{dB} \] In general, when dealing with the RF parts and circuits, we can consider this table as the rule of thumb to understand the information that such parts usually provide in their data sheet.
