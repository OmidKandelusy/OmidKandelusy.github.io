Digital Fourier Signal Processing

Frequency domain analysis is a powerful tool that enables engineers and scientists to readily investigate and predict behavior of dynamic systems like the communication networks. The frequency domain analysis via Fourier transforms and series is arguably the earliest efforts to translate a system from the time domain into a more tractable framework. Now we have many transforms such as Laplace, Z, wavelet, Hilbert, to name a few. Each of these transform spaces have their own limitations such a convergence criterion that makes them attractive for certain problem setups. The Fourier space is particularly exciting as it can be used for the steady state sinusoid regimes which essentially encompasses a wide variety of the dynamic systems that we are dealing with in everyday life from wireless communication systems to electronic audio appliances and to mechanical systems. However, there are some nuances involved in using computers to do the Fourier space analysis which I would like write a few paragraphs here about it. Let's dive into the magical world of digital signal processing!

Sampling and digitalization!

The digital signal processing of the real time analogue signals pivots around the sampling and quantization of the original continuous signal. A typical ideal analogue to digital convertor with a sampling frequency of \(F_s\) (Hz) or sampling period of \(T_s\) (Seconds) is usually mathematically expressed by the convolution of impulse train and an analogue signal \(g(t)\) as \[ \tilde{g}[n] := \hat{g}(nT_s) = g(t) * \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta\left(t-kT_s\right) \quad \text{where } n=-\infty:\infty\] Arranging the definitions for the continuous and discrete Fourier transforms, it is straightforward to show that the discrete time Fourier transform (DTFT) of \(\tilde{g}[n]\) can obtained from the continuous transform as \[ \tilde{G}(e^{j\omega}) = G\left(j\Omega\right) \Bigg|_{\Omega = \frac{\omega}{T_s}} \longrightarrow \quad \tilde{G}(e^{j\omega}) = \frac{1}{T_s} \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} G\left( j\left( \frac{\omega}{T_s} - \frac{2k\pi}{T_s} \right) \right) \] where \(\omega\) is the angular frequency for the discrete transform domain and \(\Omega\) the continuous counter part. There are several important nuances that can be inferred from this equation relating the continuous frequency domain to the angular and periodic frequency domain \(\omega\) that can be discussed easily through the simple example below. Thus, we can map the frequency-domain signal processing of the continuous signals onto the discrete domain which can be done via computers and digital computation. Evidently, in order to avoid aliasing and signal distortion, we have the following well known Nyquist sampling criterion to adhere to \[T_s\omega_0 < 2\pi - T_s\omega_0 \longrightarrow \boxed{F_s > 2\omega_0}\] However, as the discrete time fourier transform of the sampled signals shows, the frequency domain transformed signal is a continuous function of the angular frequency \(\omega\): \[ X(e^{j\omega}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]e^{-j\omega n }\] This continuous signal is a problem when it comes to digital processing in the frequency domain.

DFT instead of DTFT

To address this challenge, we use the discrete time Fourier transform or DFT in abbreviated form. DFT is a sequency of complex values at specific multiplication of a base frequency. In other words, the DFT is just a tool to enable digital processing in the frequency domain. The DFT revolves around the relation between the discrete Fourier series coefficients and DTFT of the periodic signals. Long story short, if we take a given finite discrete signal \(x[n]\) and assume it is periodic with a period of \(N\), we can define the DFT for the periodic version \(\tilde{x}[n]\) as \[ X[k] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-j2\pi k \frac{n}{N}} \quad \text{where } k=0,..., N-1 .\] Here, we can easily build the frequency axis with digital computation as it is just integer multiplication of a based frequency bin value given by \(1/N\). Now, the only question left to answer is that how the DFT frequency bins that build up the DFT of the signal relate to the sampling frequency that is used for quantization of the original analogue signal.

DFT and sampling frequency

This is a very important nuance which is commonly neglected in text books and tutorials. As mentioned above, the physical frequency axis of the original continuous signal and the sampled signal is related as \(\Omega = \frac{\omega}{T_s}\). Therefore, each frequency bin of the DFT corresponds to \(\frac{F_s}{N}\). It follows that the DFT of the signal must contain both sides of the negative and positive frequency contents, but laid out as integer multiplication of the base frequency bin as \[\text{physical frequency :=} \frac{kF_s}{N}, \quad \text{where } k=0,...,N-1\] Here, since the DTFT frequency axis is limited to \([0, 2\pi]\), based on the fact that \(N\) is even or odd, the first half of the DFT sequence members belong to the positive frequency range inclusively, and the rest to the negative range. This particularly important, when we want to accurately represent the continuous Fourier transfer of the original signal via the DFT.
